How to make video ads for your app

Most app ads lose the viewer on the first line. This is the method behind every ad ForgeLabs App Ad Studio makes: what to say first, what to show, and how to tell which ad actually worked.

By Hassan Omar · 10 November 2026

Four ideas the whole method rests on

  1. The hook does most of the work. The first line decides whether anyone sees the rest, so it gets the most care.
  2. Show the real app. Your actual screens, never a mock-up that promises more than the app does.
  3. Change one thing at a time. Ads in a test share everything except the hook, so the result tells you which hook won.
  4. Make variants cheap. If a new hook takes a day in an editor, you'll test two. If it takes minutes, you'll test ten.

Write a hook that passes the clarity test

A hook passes if a stranger can picture the app, or feel the problem, within about two seconds of hearing it. Clever, abstract or poetic lines usually fail, even when they sound good in a meeting.

The strongest pattern is the familiar comparison: "X, but for Y". The viewer already knows how X works, so the whole app lands at once.

"It's like Tinder, but for stocks." 5.8% click-through on 1,784 impressions, the best of StockMatcherAI's ads with over 1,000 impressions.
"Your money is already saying something. Is it what you believe?" Sounds good. Doesn't say what the app is. 1 click from 79 impressions.

Both ran in the same campaign for the same app. The full numbers are in the StockMatcherAI case study.

Hook types worth testing

Write a few hooks of each type, then test the best of each against each other.

TypePattern
Comparison"It's [something familiar], but for [your thing]."
Problem"Everyone says [common advice]. Nobody explains [the hard part]."
Callout"You've been about to start [thing] for [time] now."
Values"You'd [do something good]. Could you name one [way your app helps]?"
Curiosity"Nobody ever explained what [jargon] actually means."

Keep every hook true

  • Check every claim against the app as it is today. Every number, feature and result.
  • Never imply the app knows something it doesn't. "Where your money's been going" implies access to someone's bank account. If the app can't see it, the hook can't say it.
  • Describe the situation, not the person. Ad platforms restrict ads that assert things about the viewer, such as their health, finances or beliefs. "Are you struggling with debt?" is a classic rejection.

Build the ad around the hook

PartLengthWhat's on screen
Hook2–4 sThe line, large, word by word as it's spoken, with the app already moving on frame one.
Body10–15 s3–5 short lines, each paired with the app doing exactly that thing. Show the solution; don't restate the problem.
Call to action3–4 sWhat to do and where, said and shown as a button: "Download [app] free on the App Store."

Make it vertical (9:16, 1080×1920) for Reels, TikTok and Shorts, and caption every word. Many people watch with the sound off, so the captions have to carry the message alone.

Test it fairly

  1. Test 3 to 5 hooks at a time, all with the same body, format and targeting.
  2. Give each about 1,000 impressions before calling a winner on click-through.
  3. Don't edit the ads while the test runs. Every change can reset the platform's learning.
  4. Compare click-through (link clicks ÷ impressions) between your own ads, not against industry averages.
  5. Replace the weakest with new hooks in the winning style, and go again.

Platforms shift budget to ads that do well early, so some ads get very little spend. An ad with almost no impressions hasn't lost; it hasn't been tested yet.
