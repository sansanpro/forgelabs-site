Hooks

App ad hook examples

The hook is the first line of a video ad, and most viewers decide in about two seconds whether to keep watching. Here are eight hook types for app ads, a pattern to fill in for each, and real hooks with the results they got on Meta.

By Hassan Omar · 20 November 2026

The one test every hook has to pass

A stranger should be able to picture the app, or feel the problem, within about two seconds of hearing the line. If they can't, the rest of the ad never gets a chance.

These two hooks ran in the same campaign, for the same app:

"It's like Tinder, but for stocks." Passes. 5.8% click-through on 1,784 impressions.
"Your money is already saying something. Is it what you believe?" Fails. It sounds good but never says what the app is. 1 click from 79 impressions.

Eight hook types, with examples

The real hooks below are from StockMatcherAI, an iPhone app that matches people with companies working on causes they care about. Its ads ran on Facebook and Instagram from 5 to 10 November 2026; the numbers are link click-through (link clicks ÷ impressions). Where a type had no real ad, the examples are written for an imaginary habit-tracking app, to show the shape.

1. Comparison

Explains the app through something the viewer already knows. Usually the strongest type, because the whole app lands at once.

Pattern: "It's like [an app everyone knows], but for [what yours does]."

  • "It's like Tinder, but for stocks." Real: 5.8% click-through on 1,784 impressions, the best of the ads with over 1,000.
  • "It's Tinder, except the matches have revenue." Real: a twist on the same comparison.
  • "It's like Duolingo, but for your habits." Example.

Another brand's name is fine as a comparison. Leave out their logo and colours.

2. Problem

Names a frustration the viewer already has, so they nod before they know what the app is.

Pattern: "Everyone says [common advice]. Nobody explains [the hard part]."

  • "Everyone says just invest. Nobody explains in what." Real: 4.9% click-through on 1,558 impressions.
  • "Analysis paralysis isn't research. It's fear with a spreadsheet." Real: 6.0% click-through on 766 impressions, the highest rate of the ads with over 500 impressions, though on fewer impressions than the leaders.
  • "Everyone says build better habits. Nobody explains how to keep one past week two." Example.

3. Outcome

Shows the result the viewer wants, minus the part they dread.

Pattern: "[The result people want] without [the usual pain]."

  • "A daily habit you actually keep, without ten reminder alarms." Example.

Outcome hooks are where invented claims creep in. Promise only what the app does today.

4. Anti-gatekeeping

Says the field was made to sound harder than it is, and that the app cuts through it.

Pattern: "[Experts] made [your field] sound hard on purpose."

  • "Productivity gurus made habits sound hard on purpose." Example.

5. Callout

Describes the viewer's own behaviour back to them. It must describe what they do, never who they are.

Pattern: "You've been meaning to [goal] for [time] now."

  • "You've been about to start investing for three years now." Real: 3.8% click-through on 613 impressions.
  • "You've been meaning to start running since January." Example.

6. Curiosity

Opens a question the ad then answers. It works when the answer is the app.

Pattern: "Nobody ever explained what [jargon] actually means."

  • "Nobody ever explained what habit stacking actually means." Example.

7. Values

Ties the app to something the viewer cares about, then shows the gap the app fills.

Pattern: "You'd [do something good]. Could you name one [way to do it]?"

  • "You'd donate to fight cancer. Could you name one company working on it?" Real: 3.4% click-through on 1,733 impressions.
  • "You'd read more if you had the time. Could you name the last book you finished?" Example.

8. Seasonal

Rides a moment in the calendar. Strong in season, stale a week later, so only run it then.

Pattern: "New year, new [thing]."

  • "New year, new habits. Start with one." Example.

Hooks that fail, or get rejected

HookProblem
"Your money is already saying something."Abstract. It doesn't say what the app is, so the viewer scrolls on.
"Are you struggling with debt?"Asserts something about the viewer's finances. Ad platforms restrict this; describe the situation, not the person.
"See where your money's been going."Implies the app can see the viewer's bank account. If the app can't see it, the hook can't say it.
"Guaranteed to fix your habits, 10x faster."Promises a result no app can guarantee, with a number nobody measured.
"The #1 app for investors."A superlative you can't prove.

Rules for every hook

  • One idea, about three seconds out loud. Usually 12 words or fewer.
  • Every claim true today. Every number, feature and result. Sample data in your screenshots isn't a claim you can quote.
  • Plain spoken English. Read it aloud. If it sounds like a slogan, rewrite it as a sentence.
  • Same body behind every hook in a test. Then the result tells you which hook won, not which edit.

Test hooks against each other

Pick the best three to five, across different types, and run them with the same body, format and targeting. Give each about 1,000 impressions before calling a winner, then replace the weakest with new hooks in the winning style.
